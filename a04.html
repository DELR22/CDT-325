Section 01

Introduction

In Assignment 03, you decided how the building works. Now you draw it, increasing the Level of Detail (LOD). Design Documents are where a scheme becomes an instrument other people can read and act on, where a zone becomes a room with a name, a number, and an area; where a corridor becomes two walls with a rating; where a block that held a bed becomes a bed someone can get in and out of. This is also where the semester’s research either shows up or disappears. Everything before this argued about what the interior space should do. This assignment takes concepts and brings them closer to reality.

Section 03

A Warning Before You Begin

Assignment 04 is built directly on the results of Assignment 03. Stage 1 inherits your block plan’s geometry and allows for non-substantial edits and adjustments; Stage 2 exposes the logic by which the project becomes life- and fire-safe. A Block Plan with an unresolved egress path, a missing compartment line, or a mixed public–resident circulation will carry those problems into both stages.

If your Block Plan comes with deficiencies, fix them first. Students who begin A04 on a sub-optimal A03 Block Plan will, inevitably, do the work twice: once to draw a flawed document, and again to correct it. That is not a penalty; it is how the design process works.

The Main Objective Is to Produce Design Documents

What must be produced in this assignment shows that the design is fully resolved. Walls with ratings, doors that swing correctly, furniture that proves the room works, fixtures with clearances. The documents must include notes and dashed clearances rather than dimensions. The basic FF&E included is not for a contractor to build from, but as a reference for scale, proportion, and functionality, so don’t waste time finding excessively detailed blocks or families to develop Floor Plans.

Design Documents are a set that a client approves and a consultant coordinates against. Your job as an interior designer is to include all pertinent information so the project can proceed to a more definitive stage.

Section 04

Objectives & The Tools

Objectives

  1. Translate a block plan into accurate, code-compliant interior design floor plans.
  2. Draw partitions, openings, and doors that carry their code performance as drawn information.
  3. Demonstrate spatial functionality through FF&E, casework, and plumbing fixtures with real clearances.
  4. Communicate compliance through room tags, clearances, and annotations rather than dimensions.
  5. Translate the life-safety logic of the plan into a color-coded overlay that a reviewer could check.
  6. Sustain the design concept and the user research from schematic zoning into a resolved design.

The Tools

  1. Your Block Plan from Assignment 03: corrected and every functional issue resolved.
  2. The Concept Statement your firm (team) produced in Assignment 02.
  3. The Primary and Secondary Source Research from Assignment 01, with the design insights and collective knowledge from the course’s video repository.
  4. The Area Program and the code requirements from Assignment 03 remain unchanged and are now drawn and specified rather than diagrammed.
  5. The project will be drawn using AutoCAD or modeled in Revit, as your instructor directs for your section.
  6. When using AutoCAD:
    • An AutoCAD base drawing will be provided with all the layers needed to develop the Design Documents, basic furniture blocks, dimensions, hatches, lettering, and 48″x36″ layouts with title blocks. You must adhere to the content of this file, as it must be used as a template. No additional layers will be allowed to enter the file, so be sure to use the PURGE command to clear unwanted layers.
    • CTB files, one per Stage, will be provided to ensure accurate line widths and general graphic standards. Using layers not native to the provided template will cause issues when plotting.
    • The CTB files must be uploaded into the Plot Styles folder by using the PLOTTERMANAGER command and selecting the PLOT STYLES folder.
  7. When using Revit:
    • A Revit Project Template will be provided. It will include the corresponding View templates for both stages, all required Revit Families, and 48″ x 36″ Sheets with title blocks.

Section 05

Stage 1 · Interior Design Floor Plans

Sheet 1 · First and Second Floors · Scale 1/8″ = 1′-0″

AWalls & Partitionsthickness and rating+
  • Every wall in the project must be between 4″ and 6″ thick. Walls < 4″ are not allowed.
  • Fire-rated partitions must be at least 5″. A 4″ wall, for this project, cannot be rated.
  • A 5″ or 6″ wall may or may not be rated. You cannot read a wall’s rating from its thickness, and neither can anyone else. It is why the rating has to be carried by notes on this sheet and by color on Sheet 2, not by the drawing of the wall itself.
  • To add notes and specify wall assemblies that are fire-rated, refer to USG Wall Assembly types.
BWindows & Glass Partitionsglazing and fire rating+
  • Draw all exterior windows and interior glass partitions based on the activities occurring in the interior space.
  • Interior glazing in a rated assembly must not be free of notes. If glass partitions are placed, they must include a fire rating if required (assembly spaces).
  • To add notes and specify glass partitions that are fire-rated, refer to Fireglass fire-rated products.
CDoorsswing, width, and clearance+

Draw every door with its swing, at the correct doorway-equivalent width, complying with the project and code requirements:

  • 42″ minimum at resident bedrooms (gurney access).
  • 36″ minimum elsewhere, with the leaf drawn at true width.
  • Exit doors swing in the direction of egress. Assembly doors swing out without intruding into the corridor.
  • Interior doors project no more than 7″ into a corridor.
  • Barrier doors at each compartment line: a pair at 42″, each swinging in the opposite direction from the other.

A door swing is not a graphic convention here. It is the drawing telling you whether the plan works.

  • Inward-swinging doors always swing against a wall or a back surface, never into the space.
  • Outward-swinging doors may not swing more than 7″ into corridors, as they must not block egress paths.
  • Make sure doors are placed at least 4″ from a back wall.
  • Doors must allow for a space of 12″ from the push side and 18″ from the pull side.
  • Door pockets must be wider than the door opening to allow for these accommodations.
DFF&E, Casework & Fixed Systemsat true size, no exceptions+
  • Fully furnished floor plans. Use loose furniture, cabinetry, and fixed furniture systems (templates, blocks, or families) that establish location and prove the space functions.
  • Every space in the program gets furnished, including the ones that are easy to skip: the country kitchens, the spa/bathing rooms, the classrooms, the student commons.
  • Draw furniture at true size to the required 1/8″ scale. Do not “cheat” and scale furniture to make the space “work.” Doing this constitutes lying to the client and final users: just because smaller furniture is drawn does not mean that real furniture will fit or that the space will function.
EPlumbing Fixturestrue size, ADA clearances+
  • Draw all toilets, sinks, vanities, and showers at true size with ADA-compliant clearances, drawn with dashed lines. For reference, visit the ADA Guide, Chapter 6: Toilet Rooms.
  • 5′-0″ turning circle in each accessible toilet room.
  • Toilet centerline 18″ from the wall — 36″ and 42″ perpendicular grab bars attached to the wall, 56″ x 60″ clearance.
  • Roll-in shower with 30″ × 60″ clear space.
  • Sinks with 30″ × 48″ clear floor space in front.
FClearancesdrawn general, noted specific+
  • Draw the general clearances. Turning circles, clear floor spaces, maneuvering clearances at doors, and corridor widths.
  • Note the details. Grab bar heights, knee and toe clearances, mounting heights, and reach ranges cannot be drawn at an 1/8″ scale.
GRoom Tagsname, number, area+
  • Every space carries a tag with: Space Name, Space Number, Area in SF.
  • Use a floor-prefixed numbering convention: 101, 102, 103 on the First Floor; 201, 202 on the Second.
HAnnotations & Noteseverything the drawing cannot show+

Notes carry everything the drawing cannot. At minimum:

  • Code compliance notes: under-counter and knee clearances, mounting heights.
  • Partition notes: which walls are fire-rated and their rating.
  • General notes: occupancy classification key.
  • Anything a reviewer would otherwise have to guess.

Section 06

Stage 2 · Fire & Life-Safety Overlay Plans

Sheet 2 · First and Second Floors · 1/8″ = 1′-0″

Same plans, different job. Stage 1 says what the building is. Stage 2 says what happens when something goes wrong, and people need to evacuate.

The underlay. Your Stage 1 floor plans, screened to halftone (grey scale) to present, legible, and clearly background. Everything you add sits on top in full weight and color. This is a drawing-hierarchy exercise as much as a code exercise. The reviewer needs the building as context and the life-safety information as content. If your underlay competes with your overlay, the sheet has failed regardless of its contents.

What Must Appear

  • Occupancy data — in a color-coded legend on the sheet. Occupancy classification per zone. A reduced-scale diagram in the same sheet that uses color conventions to specify occupancy classification for specific and general areas of the building.
  • Rated construction — color-coded by rating. 1-hour fire barriers and wall types are differentiated by rating. This is where the rated/non-rated distinction becomes visible.
  • Egress and Exit-access travel path to the most remote point, dimensioned.
    • Direction-of-travel arrows
    • Dead-end corridors with lengths
    • Egress corridor widths
    • Compartment doors
  • Detection & alarm.
    • Smoke detectors
    • Manual pull stations
  • Notification — fire alarms.
  • Suppression & response.
    • Fire extinguishers
    • Fire department connection
  • Signage & lighting.
    • Exit signs and directional exit signs
    • Emergency egress lighting

Legends & Symbology

The color code scheme will result from applying the corresponding CTB (in AutoCAD) or the correct View Template (in Revit). This will guarantee a consistent color system across both floors.

Use the conventional symbology provided in the AutoCAD and Revit template files. Do not invent symbols.

A life-safety plan is read by someone who did not design the building and has no interest in your intentions. Draw for that person.

Section 08

Evaluation Rubric

The assignment is evaluated across five criteria totaling 500 points. Spatial Translation, Functional Resolution, and the Fire & Life-Safety Overlay each carry the greatest weight, as they are the core technical work of the Design Documents.

Criterion Excellent Proficient Developing
1. Spatial Translation & Code-Compliant Construction 125 pts total
113 – 125 pts
Translates the block plan with rigor: resolves partition, opening, and door placement by specifying wall thicknesses and rated assemblies, glazing appropriate to its activity, and swings that satisfy width, clearance, projection, and direction of egress throughout.
88 – 112 pts
Translates the block plan competently: resolves partition, opening, and door placement by satisfying the essential requirements while leaving isolated conflicts in thickness, rating, glazing, or door swing.
0 – 87 pts
Translates the block plan loosely: resolves partitions and openings by disregarding thickness or rating, and by drawing doors whose width, clearance, or swing obstructs egress or conflicts with code compliance.
2. Functional Resolution & Accessibility 125 pts total
113 – 125 pts
Furnishes the project honestly: proves every space functions by placing FF&E, casework, and plumbing fixtures at true size, and by drawing turning circles, clear floor spaces, and maneuvering clearances that demonstrate the building serves the people who will occupy it.
88 – 112 pts
Furnishes the project adequately: proves the principal spaces’ function by placing FF&E and fixtures at true size and drawing the required clearances, leaving secondary spaces thinly furnished or with unresolved clearances.
0 – 87 pts
Furnishes the project superficially: asserts a function by omitting furniture, scaling it to fit, or neglecting the clearances, producing rooms that their intended occupants could not use.
3. Fire & Life-Safety Overlay Documentation 125 pts total
113 – 125 pts
Documents life safety with rigor: makes the building’s logic checkable by color-coding rated construction and occupancy, dimensioning the travel paths, and locating detection, notification, suppression, signage, and egress lighting.
88 – 112 pts
Documents life safety correctly: makes the logic legible by color-coding construction and occupancy and locating the required devices and egress information, with isolated omissions.
0 – 87 pts
Documents life safety superficially by omitting the travel path, the ratings, the occupancy data, or the required devices.
4. Technical Standards & Drawing Discipline 75 pts total
68 – 75 pts
Works to graphic standards and produces consistent design documents by holding to the provided templates, layers, and plot styles.
53 – 67 pts
Works to graphic standards and produces reliable design documents by holding to the template, though with stray layers, uneven line weights, or misuse of plot styles.
0 – 52 pts
Disregards graphic standards and produces unreliable design documents by introducing foreign layers or ignoring the plot styles.
5. Concept Continuity & Design Statement 50 pts total
45 – 50 pts
Sustains the concept through resolution: accounts for the design by demonstrating that the project’s concept remains legible in the drawn plan and by tracing specific decisions to the primary and secondary research behind them.
35 – 44 pts
Sustains the concept: accounts for the design by connecting the plan to the project’s concept and research, while asserting the link rather than demonstrating it.
0 – 34 pts
Abandons the concept: accounts for the design by summarizing what was drawn rather than why it was drawn, leaving the plan unconnected to the project’s concept.
