Section 01

Context, Guiding Principle & Objectives

Retirement is no longer synonymous with withdrawal. A growing typology — sometimes called a university-based retirement community, sometimes a lifelong learning residence — houses adults aged 60 and older in a setting where living and learning share a roof. Residents attend classes, teach them, use libraries and studios, and remain intellectually and socially active. The building must therefore do two things at once: support aging bodies with dignity, and support the life of the mind with vigor. These are not the same requirement, and they are frequently in tension.

This is the first step in a multi-part sequence culminating in the design of a residence and learning center for a 60+ community within an existing building. Later assignments carry you into programming, space planning, and design. Assignment 01 is the empathize-and-discover foundation beneath all of it. Your task here is not to design anything. It is to understand — firsthand and accurately — the people you will design for, and to build, collectively, a shared body of knowledge every subsequent assignment will draw from.

Guiding Principle

“Nothing about us without us.” The 60+ community members you speak with are not subjects to be studied, and they are not objects of sympathy. They are the primary authorities on their own lives and the co-informants of your design. Older adults are the population most consistently designed for and least consistently designed with. This assignment exists to correct that in your practice, permanently.

Be alert to your own assumptions. Aging is not a single condition, and a 62-year-old professor, a 78-year-old retired engineer, and an 89-year-old widow do not constitute one user group. Nor does age imply decline: many of your interviewees are more capable, more discerning, and busier than you are.

Objectives

  1. Conduct primary-source research through direct interviews with members of the 60+ community, centering their expertise about their own lives.
  2. Conduct secondary-source research to locate the theories, precedents, and standards relevant to your group’s assigned topic.
  3. Synthesize primary and secondary research — testing what the literature claims against what you actually heard — rather than reporting either in isolation.
  4. Practice the ethical conduct of human research, including informed consent, participant privacy, and respectful, non-extractive engagement.
  5. Contribute your findings to a collective, crowdsourced library that becomes shared source material for the residential design project to come.

Engagement Group Structure & Community Engagement

You will work in groups of four. Each group is assigned one topic from the topic bank. Each group must conduct four interviews — one per group member. Every student is individually responsible for recruiting, scheduling, conducting, and documenting one interview with a member of the 60+ community. No student may conduct zero interviews; no student may conduct all four.

  1. Recruitment. You are encouraged to reach out to relatives, friends, neighbors, former teachers, or acquaintances aged 60 or older — the people most likely to speak with you candidly and at length. If you do not have such a contact, notify the instructor early. Do not leave this to the final week.
  2. Format. Interviews are conducted remotely, by video call or text-based messaging, at the participant’s convenience and by whichever method the participant prefers. Meet the participant where they are comfortable; do not push someone onto a platform they dislike using.
  3. Coordinate as a group. Although each of you interviews separately, your four interviews are one dataset. Agree in advance on which script questions each of you will ask so the group covers its topic with range rather than four people asking the same three things. Debrief together immediately after — the patterns across your four conversations are where this assignment’s value lies.
  4. Eligibility. Participants must be 60 years of age or older and must be able to give informed consent for themselves.
    • May not interview anyone who cannot consent on their own behalf
    • May not interview anyone with a legally authorized representative or guardian
    • May not interview anyone residing in a nursing facility, memory-care setting, or hospital
    • Any doubt about a prospective participant’s capacity to consent — do not proceed, bring it to the instructor

Ethics Research Compliance & Ethics

Informed consent. Instead of a signed multi-page form, participants give informed consent verbally or in writing at the very start of the interview, using the script that matches your format. It is short enough for a one-time conversation but still covers everything a participant needs to know before agreeing to take part.

Consent must be obtained before any interview question is asked, and it must be documented. For a video call or phone interview, that means an audio recording, a video recording, or a written transcript of the consent exchange. For a text-message interview, the participant’s written reply in the chat is the documentation — save the thread.

Section 03

The Two Research Tracks

Primary Source

Original, firsthand evidence you collect yourself, directly from the people or situation you are studying — with no one else’s interpretation layered in between. Here, that’s your four interviews: what participants say and do, and your own notes and observations from those conversations.

Secondary Source

Analysis, synthesis, or reporting about a topic produced by someone else, usually removed from the original source. Here, that’s peer-reviewed studies, codes and standards, evidence-based design and environmental gerontology literature, and built precedents on your assigned topic.

Primary-source research. Through your four interviews, learn directly from members of the 60+ community. Capture what you hear through audio, notes, and authorized photography. The goal is genuine understanding of how these individuals experience the built environment — how space supports or obstructs independence, learning, comfort, dignity, and belonging — not a checklist of “needs.” Listen for what people do, not only what they say they want, and for the workarounds they have stopped noticing they perform.

Secondary-source research. Investigate your assigned topic through credible published sources: peer-reviewed research, design standards and codes, evidence-based design and environmental gerontology literature, and built precedents. Establish what theory and best practice claim about your topic as it relates to residential and learning environments for this population.

The Central Requirement

The Grounded Design Insight

The intellectual work lives in the collision between the two tracks: where does the lived experience you documented confirm, complicate, or contradict what the literature claims?

Each video must present at least one Grounded Design Insight: a specific, defensible claim your group could only make because you conducted these interviews — explicitly linking a primary-source finding to a concrete design implication for the future residence and learning center.

The test is simple, and it is how the videos will be judged: could someone reach this claim by searching online, without conducting your interviews? If yes, it is regurgitation and does not count. If not, the claim depends on lived experience you gathered firsthand — it is an insight. The strongest insights come from the moments where what you heard confirmed, complicated, or outright contradicted what the literature claimed.
