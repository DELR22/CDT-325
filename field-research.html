Section 01

Overview

To complement the course’s overall pedagogical arc and assignments, students, working in groups of four, must complete a series of Field and Research Challenges. These challenges must be completed during class time, and deliverables must be submitted via Blackboard within the next 24 hours.

The evidence that a challenge was performed during class time will be monitored by uploading photographic evidence to a collaborative board on a Miro-like platform.

The Five Field Challenges

Each challenge is worth 40 points. Open each section for the full instructions and deliverables.

1The Anthropometric Challenge40 points+
  1. Research and understand the concepts of percentile, mean, and average.
  2. Measure 10 people: 5 males and 5 females. Seek a diverse sample in terms of age, height, and body composition. Obtain the following measurements per individual:
    • Height
    • Waist circumference
    • Hip circumference
    • Mid-upper arm circumference
    • Upper arm length
    • Upper leg length
  3. Using the tables from the “Anthropometry Measurement Summary Table” (alabama.box.com/s/w4cdoxusrkd63dfi4aek4k5ug4jhai9e), locate where the measured individuals fall within the established percentiles.
  4. Calculate the Mean of your sample.
  5. Create a single slide that includes all the data you obtained, classifying your samples by sex and percentile, and the calculated means.
Deliverables

Photographs of the measurement activities, uploaded to the Collaborative board.

The single data slide, loaded to the Collaborative board.

2The Accessibility Challenge40 points+

Groups take turns using a wheelchair, completing a circuit on the University’s campus. Throughout the circuit, one person must be seated and operating the wheelchair, while others assist with navigation. All must record the journey by taking photographs, timing it, and noting the difficulties or facilities encountered along the round trip. Be sure to notice design cues that guide you on your journey.

  • All routes begin and end at the classroom door.
  • Since there is only one wheelchair, all paths must be completed within 10 to 20 minutes so the next group can do the activity.

3The Bathroom Challenge40 points+

Measure and record on ¼″ graph paper four restrooms, hand-drawn to ¼″ scale. The drawings must include:

  • Dimensions.
  • Wall thickness — assume bounding walls are 6″ thick. Measure the thickness of the bathroom stalls and intermediate walls.
  • Accurate fixture placement.
  • ADA-compliant clearances.

Measure the following four bathrooms:

  1. The ADA single-person restroom on the ground floor of Doster.
  2. The 2–3 stall restroom on the 3rd floor of Adams.
  3. The men’s or women’s bathroom on the 3rd floor of Drummond Lyon.
  4. A public multi-user restroom — the men’s or women’s restroom on the first floor of Adams.

Research ADA guidelines and compare them to your on-site measurements and drawings.

Deliverables

Photographs of the measuring activities, uploaded to the Collaborative board.

A single slide with a comparative analysis between ADA guidelines and the bathrooms you measured and drew, loaded to the Collaborative board.

5The Accessible Furniture Challenge40 points+

Find, in the University:

  • Two pieces of furniture — workstations, drafting tables, workshop tables, or reception counters — that allow people in wheelchairs to use them comfortably.
  • Two pieces of furniture of the same categories that do not allow people in wheelchairs to use them comfortably, or at all.

Measure and draw quick sketches with the dimensions of these pieces of furniture.

Create a single sentence describing how easy or hard it was to find the furniture, and what that tells you about the accessibility people in wheelchairs have in classrooms or office spaces.

Deliverables

Pictures of the furniture, the measuring process, and sketches, uploaded to the Collaborative board.

Section 04

The Synthesis Challenge

The Synthesis Challenge asks you to translate the field research into an argument. Working from your own field evidence, your group will build and deliver a 5-to-7-minute oral presentation that answers two questions:

  1. Is code compliance the same as good design?
  2. What happens to people when design leaves human factors and whole populations out of the equation?
The Guiding Principle Your evidence is what you measured, not what you can Google. The ADA clearances are online. The percentile tables are online. The egress formula is online. None of that is your finding. Your finding is the restroom on the 3rd floor of Adams that met the number and still didn’t work, the ten-minute wheelchair trip that a walking person makes in three, the reception counter nobody could pull up to. A presentation that recites standards is a failed presentation, however accurate. The assignment is a presentation that reports what the standards did to real bodies in real buildings on this campus.

If a slide could have been made without leaving the classroom, cut it.

What the Presentation Must Do

  1. Draw a conclusion from each challenge — but only the conclusion. You are not re-presenting five challenges. You are extracting the one thing each taught you and putting them together.
  2. Take a position on the question. “Is code compliance good design?” is not a question you report on. It is one you answer and defend with what you measured. The strongest answer is not yes or no: code is a starting point, not a limitation; it is a life-safety minimum, not a definition of dignity; it is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.
  3. Name what exclusion costs. The second question is the moral center. When design leaves a population out, a person is not merely inconvenienced — they are told, by the space, that it was not built for them. Say what your fieldwork showed you about that, concretely, without sentimentality.
Aim to Build Insights Your conclusions must be insights, not summaries. A summary is true and available to anyone. An insight is a claim your group could make only because you did this fieldwork.

“ADA restrooms have clearance requirements” is a summary.

“Three of the four restrooms we measured met every ADA clearance, and one of them was still unusable, because compliance measures the room and not the entrance to it” is an insight.

Format & Delivery

  • 5 to 7 minutes, delivered live in the third session of the semester.
  • A maximum of two speakers present, but the argument is the group’s, built from all five challenges — every member should be ready to answer questions.
  • Slides support the argument; they do not replace it. Use the field evidence already uploaded to the Collaborative board — your photographs, drawings, slides, and measurements are your visual proof.
  • This is not a research report. No literature review, no recited standards. Your voices, your evidence, your conclusion.

Section 05

Evaluation Rubric

The Synthesis Challenge is evaluated across three criteria totaling 100 points. Position & Argument and Insight, Nuance & Delivery carry the greatest weight, as they are where fieldwork becomes an argument.

Criterion (Weight) Excellent Proficient Developing
1. Synthesis of Field Evidence 30 pts total
27 – 30 pts
To synthesize the fieldwork with rigor, distill each of the five challenges into insightful conclusions, and a single body of evidence built entirely from what the group measured, observed, and photographed on this campus.
21 – 26 pts
To synthesize the fieldwork competently, draw a conclusion from each challenge and connect most of them, while leaning at points on recited standards rather than measured evidence.
0 – 20 pts
To report the fieldwork, represent the challenges one by one or substitute online content for measured evidence, leaving findings unconnected.
2. Position & Argument 35 pts total
32 – 35 pts
To argue with conviction, take a clear and defensible position leveraging measured evidence, to define concretely what exclusion costs the people a space leaves out.
25 – 31 pts
To argue adequately, take a position and support it with evidence, though the stance stays close to the expected or treats exclusion in general rather than concrete terms.
0 – 24 pts
To avoid arguing, repeat the question without answering it, assert a position the evidence does not support, or reduce exclusion to inconvenience.
3. Insight, Nuance & Delivery 35 pts total
32 – 35 pts
To deliver genuine insight, reach conclusions the group could make only because it did the fieldwork, hold the contradictions that resist commonplace, and present them professionally as one coherent argument within 5–7 minutes.
25 – 31 pts
To deliver clear conclusions, offer findings rooted in the fieldwork and present them proficiently within time, though some conclusions read as a summary and the nuance is thin.
0 – 24 pts
To deliver summaries, present claims available to anyone without the fieldwork, flatten the findings to an obvious moral, or exceed the time with a disorganized talk.
Total 100 points — The Synthesis Challenge. The five field challenges (40 pts each) are graded on completion and deliverable quality, for 200 additional points.
